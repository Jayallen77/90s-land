## The disc arrived before the internet did

An AOL promotional disc could be in the house before anybody was certain how to use it. Its packaging promised a quantity of free time, while the software offered a guided route into an online service. Installing it made the computer part of another system: an account, a dial-up connection, and a telephone line that the rest of the household might also need.

The disc’s physical presence made the proposition unusually ordinary. Getting online was something that could arrive in the mail or be picked up alongside other consumer products. The strange part came later, when the modem began making noise and the screen attempted to connect somewhere outside the room.

## An hour online could have a visible cost

Time-based pricing made the clock part of the interface even when it was not on screen. Another conversation, another message board, or another attempt to download a file could mean spending longer than planned. The decision to stay online had an immediate household meaning.

On October 29, 1996, AOL announced an unlimited-use plan priced at $19.95 per month, beginning with the December billing period. A contemporary account published that day records the change. The announcement date and the start of the new billing arrangement are separate moments, which helps explain why memories of the switch do not all point to the same afternoon.

The attraction was easy to understand. A predictable monthly price made exploring the service feel different from accumulating time. It did not make the computer, telephone service, or the rest of the connection free, but it changed one especially visible part of the calculation.

## Unlimited use still met a shared phone line

The price of an online session was only one limit. A household could still have a single telephone line. A person using it for dial-up might prevent another person from making or receiving an ordinary call. The dispute moved from the bill to the room: finish the conversation, stop the download, or disconnect for a few minutes.

The online service also had a physical network behind its inviting screens. A pricing change could encourage more people to stay connected longer, placing pressure on access capacity. The promise on the package and the experience of actually connecting were not the same thing.

That gap is part of what makes the period memorable. A service could offer an enormous world while the route into it remained stubbornly local: one computer, a modem, a wall socket, and other people with plans for the line.

## A service could feel like a place

AOL’s appeal was broader than a connection to websites. Mail, chat, and organized areas of content gave a newcomer recognizable things to do. The account and screen name could become part of a person’s social identity, even when the hardware belonged to the family.

The software and the promotional material worked together. One explained the possibility; the other tried to make taking the first step feel manageable. The disc became an object that could be saved long after its offer and software had become obsolete.

## What changed when the clock loosened

Flat-rate pricing did not invent online sociability. It made one particular barrier less conspicuous for customers choosing that plan. Staying to read one more discussion or wait for a friend could feel like a different kind of decision.

That is the useful connection between the small disc and the large pricing announcement. Both tried to make going online part of ordinary life. The first supplied a doorway; the second changed how long a person might feel able to remain on the other side.
