## What if the television was already the right screen?

The family computer was one way to imagine going online in the 1990s. WebTV proposed another: keep the television, add a set-top box, connect a telephone line, and use the living room as the starting point. The web did not have to arrive through a tower and monitor on a desk.

The service launched in the United States in 1996, with hardware offered under Sony and Philips brands. Microsoft acquired the company the following year. Those dates place it among the decade’s competing ideas about how an ordinary household might encounter the internet.

## A different room created different requirements

A television picture viewed from a sofa is not the same working surface as a computer monitor viewed from a chair. Text, menus, and navigation needed to make sense at a distance. A remote control offered a familiar object for making choices, while a keyboard could make entering messages and addresses less awkward.

The equipment made an argument about confidence as well as convenience. A person already knew how to turn on a television. A small appliance might seem more approachable than choosing, installing, and maintaining a full personal computer.

That did not make browsing identical to watching a program. The web asked the viewer to select links, wait for pages, and sometimes type. The room remained familiar while the activity demanded a more active kind of attention.

## The telephone line stayed in the picture

The change of screen did not remove the route through the telephone network. A dial-up session still involved a modem and the practical limits of a household connection. Bringing the web to the sofa did not free the phone line for another person’s call.

It also brought another activity into a shared space. The same screen might be wanted for a scheduled broadcast, a game console, or somebody else’s turn online. An appliance designed around the television had to coexist with all the reasons the television was already important.

That is what makes WebTV a useful companion to AOL’s flat-rate story. One proposal changed the cost of time online; the other changed the equipment through which the customer entered. Each addressed a real obstacle while leaving other parts of the household arrangement in place.

## An appliance rather than an empty computer

A general-purpose PC could be installed with many different applications. WebTV presented a more directed experience, with the service and device designed around a limited set of online tasks. The distinction could be an advantage for someone who wanted those tasks without taking responsibility for a broader software environment.

The limits also mattered. Websites were being made for an expanding variety of browsers and computers. A dedicated television appliance had to interpret that changing medium within the constraints of its own display and controls. Simplicity at the front did not mean the technology behind it was simple.

## One of the decade’s alternative futures

WebTV is interesting partly because it makes an apparently inevitable history look less settled. The internet was not always assumed to belong to the familiar desktop setup. Companies were testing different screens, interfaces, and ideas about what customers wanted to do.

The set-top boxes in the photograph record that proposal as actual equipment. A power button, a cable, and a remote made the future concrete. In one household, getting online might mean pulling up a chair. In another, it meant sitting down on the sofa and borrowing the television.
