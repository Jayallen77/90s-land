## What if the television was already the right screen?

The family computer was one way to imagine going online in the 1990s. WebTV proposed another: keep the television, add a set-top box, connect a telephone line, and use the living room as the starting point. The web did not have to arrive through a tower and monitor on a desk.

The service launched in the United States in 1996, with hardware offered under Sony and Philips brands. Microsoft acquired the company the following year. Those dates place it among the decade’s competing ideas about how an ordinary household might encounter the internet.

The change of screen did not remove the route through the telephone network. A dial-up session still involved a modem and the practical limits of a household connection. Bringing the web to the sofa did not free the phone line for another person’s call.

It also brought another activity into a shared space. The same screen might be wanted for a scheduled broadcast, a game console, or somebody else’s turn online. An appliance designed around the television had to coexist with all the reasons the television was already important.

That is what makes WebTV a useful companion to AOL’s flat-rate story. One proposal changed the cost of time online; the other changed the equipment through which the customer entered. Each addressed a real obstacle while leaving other parts of the household arrangement in place.

## Identify the box before the future

The launch hardware pictured here consists of WebTV-era set-top units, including Sony and Philips designs. The appliance connects the television to the service and the phone line; the television remains the display. The remote supplies navigation, with a keyboard accessory useful when entering more than a few characters.

Microsoft and WebTV’s September 1996 announcement describes browsing and email as household tasks that can reach a TV. It is a contemporary proposal, not a claim that every ordinary computer application could run on the box.

## What the sofa changes

- A link must be recognizable from farther away than a desktop monitor.
- Moving a highlight with a remote is a different action from pointing a mouse.
- Entering an email address makes the choice of input device matter immediately.
- A page designed for a computer still has to fit the television’s display constraints.

The WebTV contribution to the W3C’s later discussion of device independence describes those constraints from the platform’s own perspective. That makes it a useful source for what the design had to accommodate, rather than a reason to describe the product as simply a computer with fewer parts.
