## Before the match, move the furniture

A LAN party could begin with the least glamorous part of gaming: carrying a computer. A tower, a CRT monitor, a keyboard, a mouse, and their cables were a substantial commitment to an evening. The machine that normally stayed beneath a desk had to travel, and the destination needed enough tables, electrical outlets, and room for everyone to sit.

LAN meant local area network. Instead of depending on a distant server, the participating computers communicated across the network assembled in the room. That simple description leaves out the memorable work: identifying which cable belonged to which machine, making the software agree, and helping the last person reach the same game as everybody else.

## The network had a physical shape

There was no universal equipment list. Ethernet arrangements changed during the decade; a network built with coaxial cable presented different practical problems from machines connected through a hub. The game, the operating system, and the hardware all influenced the setup. A confident friend who understood the settings could become the most valuable person present before anyone fired a shot.

The room also exposed the computer’s supporting cast. Speakers competed with one another. A mouse needed a usable surface. A monitor could take up most of a small table. Bringing a faster machine did not guarantee a better seat, and a chair borrowed from the kitchen could become uncomfortable well before the night was over.

## Doom opened a door; Quake widened the room

Doom’s shareware release in December 1993 helped make the PC a place for fast, first-person action. Its networked play let human opponents and partners occupy spaces that had previously been experienced alone. The same familiar map became a different kind of place when another player could surprise you.

Quake followed in 1996 with a fully three-dimensional world. Its multiplayer culture belonged to a wider community of maps, modifications, technical advice, and repeated practice. Getting the game running was one accomplishment; learning a level well enough to anticipate another person’s route was another.

A LAN gathering could also make room for strategy games. Warcraft and StarCraft asked for a different rhythm from a shooter: building, gathering resources, watching the map, and wondering what the opponent was preparing. The contrast could change the noise in the room without making the competition less intense.

## You could hear the result

Local play gave an encounter an immediate audience. A surprising defeat could produce a shout from the other end of the table. Advice arrived without a headset channel. Someone walking behind a monitor could see information that a player would rather keep private.

The social advantages were also practical. A person who could not connect was visibly missing from the game, which gave everybody a reason to solve the problem. The machines were separate, but the evening was shared. Setup knowledge passed between people along with game tactics.

## The packing-up was part of it

At the end, the temporary network had to become ordinary rooms again. Cables were untangled, equipment was identified, and the heavy monitor went back into a vehicle. The effort is part of what distinguishes the memory from simply playing online.
