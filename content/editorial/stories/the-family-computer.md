## The computer had an address inside the house

Before a computer could follow its owner from room to room, it usually occupied a piece of furniture. The monitor took up the desk, the tower lived beneath or beside it, and a chair marked the place where homework, games, and going online had to happen. Using the computer meant going to the computer.

That arrangement made a single machine feel like several different things. It could be a word processor in the afternoon, a games machine after dinner, and an internet connection once someone agreed to give up the phone line. The equipment stayed in place while the household negotiated its purpose.

## One machine, several visible jobs

On a Windows 95 desktop, Paint makes a drawing, WordPad makes a document, Explorer makes a file easier to find, and the Start menu gathers routes into the programs. Those are distinct tasks even when they all happen behind the same monitor. The printout is another step, with its own paper, ink and waiting.

Earlier Windows 3.x uses Program Manager and grouped icons instead of that Start menu. The two [interface files](/archive/objects/windows-3/) and [Windows 95 exhibit](/archive/objects/windows-95-start/) keep the environments separate. Nostalgia becomes clearer when the window belongs to an identified version.

Microsoft’s Windows 3.0 arrived in 1990, followed by Windows 3.1 in 1992. Program groups and icons offered a visual way to find applications, although installing and configuring software could still require knowledge beyond the picture on screen. The desktop metaphor made a useful promise without removing every technical complication.

Windows 95, launched in August 1995, gave that promise a particularly memorable landmark: Start. A button gathered programs and system functions into one recognizable place, while the taskbar made running applications easier to see. It also created one of the decade’s small jokes: the route to shutting down began with a control labeled Start.

The change was learned through repetition. Open the menu, find the program, close the window, locate the saved file. Fluency developed through doing ordinary things often enough that the movements became familiar.

## A handoff that takes longer than changing seats

An invented shared-PC sequence can be very small: finish the document, save it in a place the next person will not confuse with their own work, close the program, then pass over the chair. Somebody launching a full-screen game changes the display completely. Somebody going online may also claim the telephone line.

The computer’s location matters because help, interruptions and observation are nearby. A family desktop is shared equipment in a physical room. The programs are personal activities competing for the same piece of furniture.

A computer lab made the machine part of a timetable. The same skill that felt private at home became something visible to classmates and teachers. Knowing how to find a program, save work, or help a neighboring student could matter as much as typing quickly.

The access was uneven. Some students had a similar computer at home; others mainly encountered one at school or in a library. That difference shaped what a lesson could assume and what a child might be discovering for the first time. There was no single childhood route into computing.
