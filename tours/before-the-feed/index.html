Six stops from the shared family computer to handmade homepages, screen names, portals, and search.

Stop 1 of 6

The family computer becomes a portal

One chair, one monitor, one phone line. If someone wanted to make a call, going online could become a negotiation. Look at the PC and the AOL disc before moving on.

A promotional disc offered a concrete first step: install the software. The room still needed cables, passwords, and somebody willing to wait.

Stop 2 of 6

Mosaic gives the web a visual front door

The first website offered text and links. Mosaic put pictures inside the page. Compare the plain document with the gray browser window and imagine choosing the next blue link.

These are collection recreations, not a claim that everybody began browsing at once. The dated CERN entry explains one decision that helped the Web spread.

Stop 3 of 6

GeoCities turns pages into rooms

Choose a background. Add a title that is probably too big. A personal homepage gave its maker small decisions to make visible; GeoCities supplied neighborhoods in which those pages could sit.

Try the homepage exhibit, then follow the directory object to see how a person might find a page like yours.

Stop 5 of 6

Screen names make presence personal

The name you wanted was taken. Add a number, choose an away message, and decide whether that lyric is too dramatic. A buddy list could turn the shared PC into a place your friends recognized.

The pager and away message solve different versions of the same question: can somebody reach you right now?

Stop 6 of 6

Portals and search begin sorting the neighborhood

A directory could suggest a category to open. A search box invited a question instead. Follow the Yahoo object toward the Google incorporation date, then look at the computer that brought color to the desk.

Google’s incorporation and the iMac’s first sale are different milestones in the 1998 calendar. Neither means the personal homepage disappeared that year.
