A web you could make your own

Blue links, a blank page, and the invitation to make a little corner of the Web.

A link could lead somewhere personal

An early personal homepage might begin with a name, a tiled background, and a list of interests that did not obviously belong together. A favorite band shared space with a pet photograph, a game walkthrough, and an explanation of why the page was still under construction. The person making it decided what deserved the next link.

The web’s origins were more practical. Tim Berners-Lee developed it at CERN to connect information, and CERN made its Web software freely available in 1993. The first site’s plain text and blue links offered a small demonstration of a large idea: one document could send its reader into another, without every destination belonging to a single publisher.

Building a page became a way to be there

Services such as GeoCities helped give personal publishing a recognizable setting during the decade. A visitor could encounter a page as a place rather than a stream: a front door, a collection of rooms, and a set of paths chosen by its owner. Learning a little HTML could make the difference between accepting a supplied arrangement and changing it.

The work had visible seams. An image could be missing because its filename was wrong. A background could make the text difficult to read. A decorative animation could be more memorable than the paragraph beside it. These were design problems, but they also revealed that somebody had made decisions about the page.

The result did not need to look professional to be distinctive. A collection of carefully chosen links could be the most useful part. A fan’s enthusiasm supplied an organizing principle that a general directory might never have attempted.

Build one tiny corner of the web

The invented “Mix Tape Planet” in the homepage exhibit starts with a heading, favorite recordings and a few links. Try adding stars, switching on the construction notice and hiding the counter. The same contents suddenly make a different first impression.

This is a museum demonstration, so its decorations change locally and the fictional owner is not a recovered person. The distinction lets you experiment without confusing a new page with somebody’s historical archive.
