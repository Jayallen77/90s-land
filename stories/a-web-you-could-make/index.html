A web you could make your own

Follow one public-domain decision into a more personal way of thinking about websites, links, and discovery.

A small page with a large invitation

The first-website exhibit can look almost impossibly plain beside a modern screen. There are words, links, and a page that explains how to find out more. Spend a moment with that restraint. The interesting part is less the decoration than the invitation: this document can send you somewhere else, and another document can send you back.

On April 30, 1993, CERN put its core Web software into the public domain. Its licensing history identifies the line-mode client, server, and common-code library. That is a specific decision about software, not a claim that every image, article, or later website became free to reuse.

A place rather than a feed

The personal-homepage recreation in this museum starts from a different question than a continuously refreshed feed. What would you put in your own corner? A favorite color, a list of interests, a photograph, a link to a friend? A page can be small and still have a point of view.

Try the homepage builder in the Before the Feed tour. Changing the stars or the appearance of the page is deliberately modest. It lets you experience a design decision directly: the screen begins to look like someone made it, even when that someone has only a few controls. The result is a recreation for exploration, not a recovered historical page.

Following a human connection

A webring is another useful object to read as a set of invitations. Next site. Previous site. Somewhere random. The route is part of the experience. A link chosen by another person can be interesting because it reflects their taste, including its oddness and its limits.

The museum’s resource directory follows that spirit while labeling external destinations. An archive, a reference work, and a playable exhibit do different jobs. Opening one is a choice about what you want next: a fact to check, an object to inspect, or a place to wander.

Keep the distinction between memory and evidence

A visitor counter or guestbook can evoke the feeling of being noticed. Here, those elements are identified as original interface recreations. The guestbook lets you preview an entry without sending or saving it. The Passport keeps optional progress in your own browser.

That distinction is part of preserving the experience honestly. A recreation can help explain what an interface felt like without pretending to be an original artifact. A source can establish a date without telling us how every visitor felt. Take the tour with both in mind: follow the evidence, then leave room for the pleasure of discovering someone else’s small corner.
