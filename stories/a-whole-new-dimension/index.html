A whole new dimension

Where do your hands go on an N64 controller? Follow the jump from flat screens to 3D spaces.

The jump needed a new kind of thumb

The first surprise in Super Mario 64 is how little you need to do. Outside Princess Peach’s castle, you can run in a circle, climb a tree, or spend an unreasonable amount of time trying to land a neat somersault. There is no side-scrolling screen pushing you toward the right. The space itself is an invitation, and the control stick turns learning to move into part of the entertainment.

Nintendo launched the Nintendo 64 in Japan in June 1996 and North America that September. Super Mario 64 supplied an unusually clear introduction to its central idea: Mario could head toward the camera as readily as away from it. Pushing the stick gently or decisively changed how he moved. The controller’s strange middle grip suddenly had a job.

The camera became another thing to learn

A platform game in three dimensions has an extra problem. Before judging a jump, you need to see where the landing is. Mario’s camera could help, but it also made the player aware of a viewpoint that was separate from the character. Sometimes the difficult part was arranging the picture before attempting the move.

That adjustment is easy to overlook now. On the Super NES, Super Mario World had already offered branching routes and hidden exits, but its familiar side view made distances legible in another way. The difference was not simply sharper graphics. A player brought old habits into a space that demanded new information: depth, orientation, and whatever was just outside the frame.

The PlayStation had reached Japan in December 1994 and North America in September 1995. Its early games offered their own lessons. Ridge Racer made a road bend toward the horizon; Tekken put fighters inside a polygonal arena. Tomb Raider, released in 1996, made ledges, chambers, and the relationship between Lara Croft’s body and a grid of space central to exploration.

These games did not all ask for the same controls. Sony’s original pad used a directional cross, while later PlayStation controllers added analog sticks. The shift happened across machines, genres, and successive designs. Owning a new console did not mean suddenly forgetting how to play a flat game, either. Two-dimensional favorites stayed on the shelf beside the experiments.

At the computer desk, Quake supplied another perspective in 1996. The player looked out from inside its world, with movement and aiming becoming linked problems. The low corridors and abrupt changes in height made the environment feel like a place to navigate, rather than a painted background behind the action.

Getting lost could be the point

The pleasures were often small: finding a room behind a waterfall, realizing a distant platform was reachable, or discovering that an apparently decorative door opened. A new viewpoint created places for designers to hide things and new ways for players to misunderstand them.

That is why a controller is a good starting point for this change. Its shape records a negotiation between the hands and the screen. The Nintendo 64 pad did not give anyone a third hand; it offered different grips for different jobs. Once the middle grip became familiar, the odd-looking device could feel natural. A whole new dimension arrived partly as a technical achievement and partly as an afternoon spent running around a castle courtyard.

Three control problems to try

Three control problems to try
Original gameA useful experimentWhat you learn
Super Mario 64, Nintendo 64Move gently with the stick, then push farther. Use the C buttons to investigate the camera.Movement speed and viewpoint are separate tools.
Tomb Raider, original PlayStationLine up Lara with a ledge before committing to the jump.The character’s facing direction and the room’s geometry matter.
Quake, original PCLook up a change in elevation before moving into it.Aiming and traveling through the space need not point the same way.

These refer to the original releases, not their remakes. Nintendo’s booklet even gives the camera an operator, Lakitu: a practical way of making the invisible viewpoint into something the player can discuss. The second thumb has not yet become everybody’s standardized solution.
