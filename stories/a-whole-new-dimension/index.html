A whole new dimension

Where do your hands go on an N64 controller? Follow the jump from flat screens to 3D spaces.

FROM THE COLLECTIONA whole new dimension

From a direction to a space

Put a thumb on the Genesis pad’s directional cross, then look at the N64 controller’s middle grip. The second object presents a small practical puzzle: which parts are you meant to hold? That puzzle is a useful way into 3D games. Moving around a space on screen meant learning a new gesture with your hands.

Now look at the Nintendo 64 controller. The extra grip and central stick interrupt the symmetry. Before a game even starts, the object asks you to reconsider where your hands belong. That physical hesitation is a useful way into the decade’s move toward three-dimensional play: familiar activities acquiring unfamiliar directions.

The dates did not arrive together

Sony’s original PlayStation launched in Japan on December 3, 1994, then reached North America on September 9, 1995. Nintendo 64 followed in Japan on June 23, 1996; Nintendo’s U.S. history records September 29 as its official American launch, with Super Mario 64.

Those separate dates matter. A memory of seeing a console in a magazine is not necessarily a memory of buying it locally. The timeline keeps each regional launch distinct so the story can include anticipation as well as arrival.

The PC was part of the change

Quake’s original 1996 release belongs in this conversation too. Bethesda and id Software celebrated its 25th anniversary on June 22, 2021. The computer desk offered another setting for exploring a 3D world, with a different set of controls and a different social arrangement around the screen.

The family-PC photograph in this archive is not a Quake launch photograph. It represents the room around that kind of play: a monitor, a desk, a chair, and the practical question of whose turn it was to use the machine. Holding those two ideas apart—a dated software release and a broader material memory—makes both more interesting.

Try reading the hardware

Open the controller objects and compare the position of a thumb, the reach to a button, and the space between the grips. Imagine moving slowly, turning around, or correcting a jump. Which action feels obvious from the shape alone? Which would need explaining?

This is an editorial reading of the objects, not a claim that one controller or console invented every part of 3D gaming. The value of the comparison is more personal: it shows how a technical change can become a learned gesture. A new dimension on screen also meant a new habit in your hands.
