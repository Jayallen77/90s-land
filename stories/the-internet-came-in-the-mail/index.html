The internet came in the mail

A promotional disc made going online look like something you could put into a computer.

An invitation you could hold

A promotional online-service disc gave an abstract network a physical entrance. The object arrived with a promise of something beyond the computer already in the house. Inserting it offered a concrete first action: install this, follow these steps and see what is on the other side.

The AOL discs in the collection show that invitation as material culture. Packaging and printed claims did part of the work of explaining why someone might want an online account before the software had begun doing anything at all.

A service is not the entire Internet

The Computer History Museum places AOL among a broader field of online services and network developments. That history is a useful correction to a common shorthand. A service could introduce a user to email, chat and information without being identical to the whole Internet or the World Wide Web.

For a new user, however, the distinction might be less immediate than the experience of getting connected. A recognizable welcome screen and a few available activities could become the practical definition of being online.

The connection had a routine

Dial-up attached the experience to a telephone line and a sequence of connection attempts. A session had a beginning that could be heard and waited through. Going online was often an activity with a clear start, rather than an invisible background condition of every device.

That made patience part of the interface. A page loaded in stages; a transfer took time; another household use of the line could interrupt the plan. The pace shaped what people chose to do once a connection worked.

The address outgrows the disc

Hotmail’s preserved July 1996 launch announcement offers another part of the story: email accessed through a browser. It illustrates how an online identity could become less closely tied to a particular service’s installed software.

The promotional disc remains interesting because it captures a moment when access needed an introduction. It was both software carrier and explanation, a small manufactured object asking a household to imagine an unfamiliar place. The network was elsewhere, but its invitation could sit on the kitchen table.
