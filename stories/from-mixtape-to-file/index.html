From mixtape to file

A personal soundtrack moved from a handwritten cassette label toward folders, filenames, and discs.

Choosing the first song

A mixtape starts with a decision about somebody else’s attention. The first track invites them in; the next one has to follow it. Unlike selecting a single favorite song, making a sequence asks how moods and tempos fit together. A cassette makes that sequence physical, with two sides and a fixed amount of space.

The label adds another layer of authorship. Handwriting, crossed-out titles and a name on the case turn a manufactured blank into a particular object. The tape is both music and a message about why these songs belong together.

Formats overlap

The 90s did not replace one listening format neatly with the next. Cassettes, compact discs and computer files could coexist in the same room. Sony’s product history places portable CD listening before the decade and MiniDisc within it, reminding us that the change was already underway and took more than one route.

Each format imposed different practical limits. A tape asked for rewinding. A disc offered direct track selection. A file required a computer, software and somewhere to store it. Convenience depended on the equipment available at the moment you wanted to listen.

A collection becomes a directory

As music entered the computer, selecting and ordering tracks acquired a new set of gestures. Names and folders took on some of the work previously done by handwriting and shelves. A CD-R could carry a sequence back out of the computer into a compatible player, making the transition feel less like a clean break than a loop.

This did not make every copy authorized, every file reliable or every player compatible. The period’s excitement about access existed alongside arguments over ownership and distribution. A format history needs room for both experiences.

The personal part survives

The common thread is the desire to say: start here, then listen to this. Whether the result was a cassette, a burned disc or a folder, the act of sequencing created a point of view. Inspect the blank cassette in the collection and imagine a title written across it. The empty label is where a mass-produced object becomes somebody’s recommendation.
