Movies & TV · FROM THE ARCHIVE

Same time next week

Appointment television made the gap between episodes part of the story.

The episode ends; the conversation starts

A scheduled television episode has an ending followed by a wait. That interval changes the audience’s relationship to a story. A cliffhanger is something to discuss, a joke becomes a shared reference and a prediction has time to circulate before the next installment arrives.

The experience depends partly on other people watching on a similar timetable. A conversation at school or work can begin with a few words because the participants already know which scene is being discussed.

A household shares one appointment

A prominent television in a shared room makes viewing a negotiation. Someone wants one program, someone else wants another, and the remote control becomes an object with unusual authority. Choosing a show can also mean choosing how the room will be used for the next half hour or hour.

The CRT in the collection is useful precisely because it is an ordinary piece of equipment. Its scale suggests furniture arranged around it, a place for the viewer to sit and a picture that several people can watch together.

Recording creates a second schedule

VHS offered a way to move a broadcast into another part of the day. That possibility still required a tape, available space and a recording that actually contained the desired program. A household could build a small library of episodes or repeatedly record over the same cassette.

Watching later changes the social timing. The program is still available, but the viewer may need to avoid a conversation until they catch up. The technology solves one scheduling problem while creating another kind of anticipation.

A screen can gather different occasions

The same television might carry a sitcom, a news bulletin, a sporting event and a film. The sourced Space Jam entry elsewhere in the archive concerns a theatrical opening, illustrating why broadcast schedules and cinema dates cannot be collapsed into one timeline of viewing.

Appointment television is best remembered as a relationship between a program and a time. The content matters, but so does arranging a day around it. The wait, the shared room and the conversation afterward are all parts of the experience that a list of episode titles alone cannot preserve.
