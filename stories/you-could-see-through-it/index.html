Tech · FROM THE ARCHIVE

You could see through it

Translucent shells made electronics feel playful, approachable, and worth looking at.

The casing becomes part of the attraction

An electronic object does not have to reveal its workings to perform its job. A translucent shell makes a different promise: there is something inside worth seeing. Even when the view is partial, the material invites curiosity rather than simply hiding the mechanism behind a neutral enclosure.

The transparent controller and Game Boy Color in this collection show how that invitation can work at a small scale. The casing remains functional, but color and visibility turn it into part of the pleasure of ownership.

A computer as a room object

Apple announced the iMac in May 1998 and positioned it around approachable Internet access. Its integrated form and distinctive translucent styling made the outside of the machine a central part of the message. The official US price in that announcement was $1,299.

The announcement and retail availability are separate dates in this archive. The distinction keeps the design’s public introduction from being confused with the moment a customer could take the machine home.

Seeing is not the same as understanding

A translucent object can make technology feel less remote without making its circuitry self-explanatory. A glimpse of components is an aesthetic encounter, not necessarily a technical diagram. That ambiguity is part of the style’s appeal: it offers a suggestion of access while preserving some mystery.

Color also shifts the way an object belongs in a room or a hand. The choice can feel personal even when the underlying function is shared with a more conventional version. Hardware becomes something to select for its presence as well as its capabilities.

A family resemblance across devices

Put the iMac, handheld and controller beside one another and a visual relationship appears despite their different jobs. Curves, colored plastic and visible interiors connect desktop computing with play. The comparison is an editorial reading of the objects, not a claim that every maker followed one origin story.

The transparent-tech collection preserves that relationship. It invites a slower look at the casing itself: what it reveals, what it obscures and how it changes the feeling of picking an electronic device up. Sometimes the future arrived as a different kind of plastic.
