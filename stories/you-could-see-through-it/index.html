You could see through it

Bondi blue on the desk, clear purple in a pocket. Take a look through the plastic.

The computer stopped pretending to be office furniture

The original iMac looked unusually at home beside a lamp, a bedroom wall, or a brightly colored notebook. Its curved Bondi blue shell made the monitor and computer one conspicuous object. A translucent surface offered a glimpse inside without requiring anyone to remove a panel or understand the circuitry.

Apple announced the machine in May 1998 and began selling it that August. It was still a computer with practical specifications: a G3 processor, USB connections, and a CD-ROM drive. Its appearance made those capabilities arrive inside an object that seemed designed to be noticed.

Color was a choice before the software loaded

Consumer electronics had never been uniformly beige, but the late-1990s interest in translucent plastic made color especially visible across computers, handhelds, and controllers. A transparent purple Nintendo 64 pad and an Atomic Purple Game Boy Color turned a familiar device into a personal selection before it was switched on.

The effect worked at more than one distance. Across a room, the color identified the object. Up close, screws, ribs, and portions of a circuit board could become part of the design. The shell both concealed the mechanism and suggested that looking through it was allowed.

A glimpse was not the same as a repair manual. A person could enjoy seeing the board without being able to identify its components. That partial visibility was part of the attraction: technology felt less anonymous, while the machine remained a finished consumer product.

The iMac also changed what was missing

Its design was not only a matter of applying colored plastic to an existing tower. The integrated shape reduced the usual separation between monitor and computer. USB became central to its connections, and the absence of a built-in floppy drive made a familiar way of moving files less convenient.

Those choices could make the machine feel refreshingly simple or immediately awkward, depending on the equipment already in the room. A beautifully coordinated desk still needed a way to deal with an old disk, printer, or accessory. The new appearance arrived alongside a practical argument about what a modern computer should include.

That is one reason the original object is more interesting than a color swatch. Its shape and connections belong to the same proposal: the computer as a complete household appliance with a recognizable personality.

Handhelds made the idea smaller

Game Boy Color arrived in 1998, putting another visible choice into a player’s hands. The shape remained a device for cartridges, buttons, and a small screen. A translucent version made its electronics part of the view, while different solid colors offered other ways to choose a favorite.

Controllers took the idea into a shared room. A distinctive color could help identify which pad was yours, even when every player used the same model. Personalization happened at the accessory level rather than requiring another console.

Why the shells still look like the period

These objects have become an efficient visual shorthand for the end of the decade because their color was structural. It was not a temporary sticker or a screen theme. It wrapped the machine, appeared around its openings, and changed how light passed through it.

The style also carried a practical kind of optimism. Computing could be complicated, but the equipment did not have to present itself as forbidding. The friendly curve and translucent shell made a promise before the startup sound: this was something you might want to bring into your room.
