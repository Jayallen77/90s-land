Before the cloud, you carried the file home

Floppies and Zip disks made the trip between computers part of the work—and compatibility part of every handoff.

Save was not the final step

A document could be safely stored and still be in the wrong place. The computer at school, the computer at work and the computer at home were separate destinations. Getting a file from one to another involved a physical medium, a compatible drive and the confidence that the copy had actually worked.

That made saving an activity with several stages. Save the current version. Copy it. Check what fits. Put the disk somewhere it will survive the trip. At the other end, find the right drive and open the right file. The modern metaphor of a shared cloud folder removes much of that visible journey; a floppy returns it to the story.

Capacity was a daily question

The Zip system offered a larger removable-disk workflow than the ordinary floppy. The Computer History Museum records the original 100 MB capacity, but its timeline’s year assignment is not enough to establish a precise retail launch here. The archive treats the pictured Zip equipment as mid-decade hardware and keeps announcement and availability claims separate.

The practical attraction is easier to establish than a universal launch day. A larger file or a group of files could travel together. Graphics work, backups and transfers could become less fragmented. The disk remained a thing with a case and a label, but the amount of work carried by that thing changed.

The drive at the other end

Removable storage creates a dependency. Owning a disk is useful only if the next machine can read it. A floppy, Zip cartridge and writable CD are not interchangeable simply because they all contain digital information. A handoff could fail before anyone reached the document itself.

That dependence made hardware part of collaboration. Before giving someone a disk, it was sensible to establish what their computer could accept. An impressive new format at one desk could be inconvenient at another. Compatibility was a social question expressed through connectors, drives and software versions.

A label could hold the whole story

Look at a removable disk as a small piece of office language. A name, a date, a project or a warning could be written directly on the object. The surface outside the computer helped explain the contents inside it. Several nearly identical disks could carry very different consequences.

This is one reason the objects remain culturally legible. Their physical labels tell us that digital work was being sorted, transported and sometimes misunderstood by hand. The file did not become less real when it left the screen; it became another object to keep track of.

A gradual change in what travel meant

Writable and rewritable CDs joined the same world of transfer and backup. The museum dates CD-RW’s introduction to 1997. A disc supplied a different combination of capacity, rewriting and compatibility, with its own limits. The decade did not settle on one permanent answer before networked storage changed the problem again.

Follow the floppy, Zip and CD-R objects into the family computer collection. Their differences explain a common 90s experience: the work might be digital, but finishing it could depend on remembering which object to put in a bag.
