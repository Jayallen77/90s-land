The console wars were fought on the couch

Your friend had a Genesis. You had a different cartridge. The argument started there.

The machine your friend had

A console rivalry became practical the moment someone handed you a cartridge that would not fit your machine. Sonic the Hedgehog belonged to Sega’s Mega Drive and Genesis world. Super Mario World belonged to Nintendo’s Super Famicom and Super NES. The advertisements could argue about speed and attitude; a household still had to choose which library would sit beneath its television.

The machines arrived on different regional calendars. Nintendo introduced the Super Famicom in Japan in 1990, with the Super NES reaching North America in 1991. Sega’s 16-bit hardware was already in the conversation. For a player, the release calendar was also a map of possible visits: who could show you Sonic, who owned Street Fighter II, and whose birthday might produce the next game everyone wanted to try.

The controller changed the argument

A Sega six-button pad makes particular sense beside the decade’s fighting games. The extra face buttons could put more actions within easy reach. Nintendo’s pad arranged its buttons differently, while the Nintendo 64 later added a control stick and an unusual three-pronged body. These were objects you learned through repeated movements, not just pictures in a buying guide.

The PlayStation’s North American arrival in 1995 shifted the furniture again. CDs, memory cards, and a growing library of polygonal games made the next purchase feel like more than a choice between familiar mascots. Nintendo 64’s four built-in controller ports made another persuasive offer: three friends could join without a separate multitap.

Bring the arcade’s vocabulary home

Street Fighter II gives this controller argument a specific shape. The arcade controls arrange light, medium and heavy punches in one row, with corresponding kicks in another. A Super NES pad distributes its six action buttons across the face and shoulders. The command still has to reach the game through the player’s hands, so a familiar move may need practice on a different layout.

The arcade guide shows the six-button grid and Ryu’s quarter-circle-forward fireball input, with facing direction made explicit. It is a normal move, not a cheat. The interesting comparison is what the player must do on each controller, rather than assuming a shared title makes every version physically identical.

Agree on the turn before the match

Winner stays on, loser passes the pad, everybody gets one race: a house rule changes the experience as surely as the console’s specifications. Four-player split-screen gives each player a smaller picture; two people sharing one controller between attempts make a different social arrangement.

The controller file is useful evidence here. An N64 pad and a Genesis six-button pad offer different tools. The argument becomes more interesting when it concerns the game in the room rather than which logo deserves to win the whole decade.
