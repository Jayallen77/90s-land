Games · FROM THE ARCHIVE

The console wars were fought on the couch

Brand loyalties, borrowed cartridges, and the household decision that shaped a whole game collection.

Picking a side

A console was a vote cast on behalf of an entire household. Once it sat beneath the television, it influenced birthday lists, rental choices and which friend brought a cartridge over. The rivalry could sound like an argument about technical specifications, but its consequences were practical: a game for one machine would not simply work on the other.

That made the playground argument unusually personal. Defending a favorite system also meant defending the games you knew best. A friend’s different machine was both the supposed opposition and a reason to visit after school.

The controller makes the pitch

The Genesis controller in this collection puts six face buttons within reach of one thumb. Sony’s original PlayStation pad uses a different arrangement, with shoulder buttons adding another layer of inputs. Nintendo 64’s three grips make its central analog stick impossible to overlook. Each object offers a different introduction to play before a screen has even switched on.

Comparing those shapes is more revealing than declaring a single winner. A fighting game, a slow exploration game and a racing game ask different things from the player’s hands. The machine’s identity was learned through those repeated gestures.

The decade changes underneath you

Sony’s Japanese launch in December 1994 and North American launch in September 1995 did not happen at the same moment. Nintendo’s official US history places Nintendo 64’s American arrival in September 1996. A household could spend much of that interval reading about hardware it could not yet buy locally.

The transition also changed what a game collection looked like. Disc cases joined cartridge boxes. Save files could travel separately from the software. A new purchase sometimes demanded learning a whole new system of storage, accessories and habits.

Remember the borrowed games

The most useful record of a console rivalry might be the title you borrowed repeatedly, rather than the machine you owned. That memory preserves the social network around the hardware: who had what, who shared it and where everybody gathered. The couch was a distribution system, a debating chamber and an arcade with familiar faces.
