THE DECADE, DAY BY DAY

1993

Dinosaurs outside. Doom after homework.

The Web’s software became public property. Wu-Tang’s debut and In Utero occupied the record shelf, while Mulder and Scully occupied Friday night. In December, astronauts set out to repair Hubble and Doom arrived on PCs.

the web gets a visual front door · 1993

NCSA Mosaic browser

About this object

Blue links, pictures inside the page, and a gray frame around the whole thing.

NCSA Mosaic gives linked documents a graphical desktop frame: menus, navigation controls and a scrollable page. Its importance is partly in that familiarity. Browsing becomes an application you can operate, alongside other programs on a computer, rather than a description of a network you have to imagine.

Charles Severance ↗ · CC0

hand-to-hand software menace · 1993

Doom shareware disks

About this object

A disk could bring a new game to a computer that usually held homework.

Doom’s shareware opening could travel as files on floppy disks before a boxed purchase. A diskette turns distribution into a physical handoff: the recipient still needs compatible hardware, setup and a working copy. The labeled collection here documents a medium, rather than establishing that every disk is the first December 1993 edition.

Pelle Wessman from Malmö, Sweden ↗ · CC BY-SA 2.0

couch-war muscle memory · 1993–1999

Sega Mega Drive / Genesis controllers

About this object

The pad carried fighting-game technique, sports-game trash talk, and console loyalty.

Two Sega controllers make the comparison here: the original Mega Drive/Genesis three-button pad above and the later six-button version below. Both put a directional cross under the left thumb. The later pad adds X, Y and Z to A, B and C, giving compatible games more face-button inputs without requiring an entirely new console.

X201 ↗ · Public domain

hand-built sequence · 1990–1997

Mixtape cassette

About this object

Pause-button edits and handwritten track lists made every tape a small act of authorship.

A compact cassette encloses magnetic tape on two reels. Playback moves that tape past a head; recording writes a new signal along it. The shell and label turn a length of sound into a portable object with two named sides.

Andrey Korzun ↗ · CC BY-SA 4.0
